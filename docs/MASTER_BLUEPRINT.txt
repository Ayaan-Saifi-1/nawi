Abstract
Non-Automatic Weighing Instruments (NAWIs) underpin fair trade, healthcare dosing, industrial control, and consumer protection across India. Before any such instrument model can be sold or verified in the field, it must clear a type/pattern evaluation against OIML Recommendation R-76, a process that today is executed largely through spreadsheets and static document templates. This manual approach is slow, inconsistent across laboratories, and vulnerable to transcription and calculation error at exactly the point where correctness matters most: the legal record of an instrument's accuracy. This document presents a complete architectural and implementation blueprint for a Digital Metrology Test Report Generation System that digitises the entire type-evaluation workflow — structured capture of instrument and environmental data, a versioned rule engine that encodes OIML R-76 permissible-error logic, automatic pass/fail determination, standardised report generation with digital signing, and a searchable, auditable repository. The design is deliberately organised around a configurable Rule Configuration Service rather than hard-coded formulae, so that future amendments to OIML R-76, or to the Legal Metrology (General) Rules, 2011, can be adopted without re-engineering the core system. Beyond the stated functional requirements of Problem Statement 26035, the blueprint proposes differentiators intended to set the system apart from conventional single-laboratory digitisation efforts: a federated multi-laboratory architecture, an offline-capable client for weighbridge and field verification sites, a tamper-evident audit trail anchored to cryptographic hashes, a public report-authenticity verification portal, and a phased roadmap toward assisted anomaly detection in test observations. The remainder of this document details the system's architecture, data model, workflows, technology stack, security posture, testing strategy, risks, and a phased implementation plan, so that any member of the receiving team can proceed directly to design and build without further independent research into the domain.

1.  Introduction
1.1  Background
 and Motivation
Weighing instruments used in trade, healthcare, industry and agriculture must produce measurements that both parties to a transaction, and the regulator, can trust. In India, this trust is established through the Legal Metrology Act, 2009 and the Legal Metrology (General) Rules, 2011, which require that a Non-Automatic Weighing Instrument (NAWI) — an electronic weighing scale, platform scale, or weighbridge that requires an operator to complete the weighing process — obtain model (pattern) approval before it is verified, stamped, and sold. Model approval is granted only after a designated laboratory has subjected a representative sample of the instrument to the type-evaluation tests specified in OIML Recommendation R-76, Non-Automatic Weighing Instruments — Part 1: Metrological and Technical Requirements, Tests.
At present, laboratories record raw test readings on paper or in spreadsheets, then manually transcribe them into Word or Excel report templates, computing permissible errors and pass/fail status by hand or with unstructured formulae. This is slow, hard to audit, inconsistent between laboratories and personnel, and difficult to update when OIML revises R-76 or when the Bureau of Indian Standards / Legal Metrology Division issues clarifications. The absence of a common digital format also makes it difficult for the Department of Consumer Affairs (
DoCA
) to maintain a national view of approved models, pending applications, or laboratory workload.
1.2  Regulatory
 Context
Legal Metrology Act, 2009 — establishes the legal framework for standards of weights and measures and the regulation of trade and commerce in weights, measures and other goods sold by weight, measure or number.
Legal Metrology (General) Rules, 2011 — prescribes model approval, verification and stamping procedures with which NAWIs sold in India must comply.
OIML R-76-1 — the international recommendation specifying metrological and technical requirements and the test procedures (administrative examination, construction check, initial examination, performance tests, influence-factor tests, and durability/endurance testing) used for pattern evaluation of NAWIs.
OIML R-76-2 — the companion pattern-evaluation report format that R-76-1 test results are traditionally reported against, and which the proposed system's standardised templates are modelled on.
Because OIML periodically revises R-76 (editions and amendments have been issued across 1976, 1992, 2006 and subsequently), and because the domestic rules may add interim provisions of their own, the system described in this document treats regulatory parameters as versioned configuration rather than as constants embedded in application code. Section 6 and Section 9 elaborate this design decision.

1.4  Objectives
Eliminate manual, spreadsheet-based computation of permissible errors and compliance status for NAWI type evaluation.
Provide a single, structured digital record per test session that is complete, validated, and traceable from raw observation to signed report.
Encode OIML R-76 requirements as versioned, auditable configuration so that revisions do not require rewriting application logic.
Produce standardised, tamper-evident test reports in both fixed (PDF) and editable (DOCX) form, matching the OIML R-76-2 reporting structure.
Give the Department of Consumer Affairs a national, searchable view of model approvals, laboratory throughput and compliance trends.
Support laboratories with intermittent connectivity (weighbridge sites, remote test centres) through an offline-capable client.
Establish security, access control and audit properties appropriate to a legal record used in regulatory decision-making.

2.  Landscape Review
2.1  Limitations
 of the Current Manual Process
Calculation risk: permissible-error and pass/fail computation performed manually or in ad-hoc spreadsheets, with no independent check on formula correctness per instrument class.
Inconsistency: report layout, terminology and level of detail vary by laboratory and by individual technician, complicating review by approving officers and by 
DoCA
.
No structured history: instrument-wise test history is scattered across files and folders rather than 
queryable
 by model, manufacturer, or date.
Weak auditability: it is difficult to reconstruct who entered which observation, when a value was changed, or why a report was revised.
Slow update cycle: when OIML issues an amendment, every spreadsheet template in use must be located and manually corrected, with no guarantee of completeness.
Limited visibility for the regulator: 
DoCA
 has no consolidated, near-real-time view of pending applications, laboratory workload, or compliance trends across the country.
2.2  Comparable
 Digitisation Efforts
Several national metrology bodies (for example, Australia's National Measurement Institute, which publishes a modified national standard and a companion procedure manual alongside OIML R-76) have long supplemented the OIML recommendation with structured, standardised evaluation-report templates and worked examples to reduce inter-laboratory variation. Digital calibration and test-management platforms used in accredited testing more broadly (ISO/IEC 17025 environments) typically combine structured data capture, versioned test methods, and electronic sign-off, but general-purpose Laboratory Information Management Systems (LIMS) are not built around OIML R-76's specific test vocabulary and permissible-error logic, and adapting one is itself a significant configuration exercise. The gap this blueprint targets is a domain-specific system — purpose-built for NAWI type evaluation under Indian Legal Metrology rules — that is as configurable as a LIMS but as precise, out of the box, as a hand-built OIML R-76 checklist.
2.3  Gaps
 This System Is Designed to Close
A single authoritative, versioned encoding of OIML R-76 permissible-error logic, rather than parallel spreadsheets maintained per laboratory.
End-to-end traceability from raw observation to signed report, satisfying audit and dispute-resolution needs.
A national, 
queryable
 repository instead of siloed local files.
A configuration-first approach to regulatory change, so future OIML editions or domestic clarifications are a data update, not a software rewrite.
Field-usable tooling for weighbridge and remote verification contexts where connectivity cannot be assumed.
3.  System Vision and Differentiation

It is to make the digital record the single source of truth for a NAWI's type-evaluation history — one that is computed once, correctly, and trusted by laboratories, manufacturers, and the regulator alike. 6.  System Architecture
6.1  Architectural
 Style
The system follows a layered, service-oriented architecture. Each functional requirement in Section 5 is served by a distinct application service; services are independently deployable and communicate through an internal API gateway. This style is chosen over a single monolithic application for three reasons specific to this domain: (a) the Compliance Engine and Rule Configuration Service must be able to evolve, and be independently version-audited, without redeploying the entire system; (b) laboratories with heavy report-generation load (PDF rendering) should not be able to degrade the responsiveness of data-entry for other laboratories, which argues for isolating report rendering into its own worker pool; and (c) a services boundary between data capture and compliance evaluation creates a natural point at which every computed value can be tagged with the rule version that produced it, which is central to the auditability goal in Section 3.

7.3  Handling
 Future OIML Revisions
No permissible-error value, accuracy-class band, or applicability rule is stored as a literal constant inside application code. Instead, each is a row in the Rule Configuration Service, keyed by an OIML edition/amendment identifier and an effective date. When an evaluation is computed, the system resolves the rule set in force at the test date (not the current date), stores that rule-version identifier alongside the computed result, and re-renders historical reports exactly as originally issued even after a newer rule set is loaded. Adopting a new OIML edition is therefore a data-loading and validation exercise — supported by a rule-authoring workflow with its own review/approval step — rather than a code change, directly satisfying the problem statement's requirement to “support future updates whenever OIML recommendations are revised.”

9.3  Compliance
 Decision Logic
For each observation, the Calculation Engine derives the observed error, resolves the applicable 
mpe
 band from the Rule Configuration Service using the instrument's declared class and the test date's effective rule version, and the Compliance Engine then applies the following decision logic:
Resolve accuracy class, e, and n = Max / e for the instrument under test.
Resolve the 
mpe
 band applicable to n and to the test stage (initial verification / type evaluation vs. subsequent verification).
Compute the observed error for the observation (e.g., indicated value minus applied reference load, corrected for zero as required by the specific test procedure).
Compare the absolute observed error to the resolved 
mpe
: within band → Pass; outside band → Fail, with the margin recorded.
Aggregate observation-level outcomes to a session-level result: a session passes only if every mandatory test type required for the instrument's category has a Pass outcome; any Fail, or any mandatory test type left incomplete, blocks report approval.
Persist the rule-version identifier, computed error, applied 
mpe
 and outcome against the observation, immutably, for audit and later reproducibility.
10.  Report Generation and Document Management
Once a test session's compliance results are complete, the Report Generation Engine auto-populates a standardised template — structured along the lines of the OIML R-76-2 pattern-evaluation report — with manufacturer and instrument details, laboratory and environmental conditions, the full observation set with computed errors and outcomes, and an overall result. The same validated data set drives two renderers: a fixed-layout PDF for legal/archival purposes and an editable DOCX for cases where an approving officer must add contextual remarks before final sign-off. Photographs and supporting documents are attached at the report level and stored in object storage, referenced rather than embedded, to keep the primary record lightweight and independently verifiable.
Every generated report receives a unique report number and a QR code linking to the public verification portal (Section 12).
A report is immutable once digitally signed; any correction is issued as a new, explicitly linked revision, never an in-place edit.
The repository supports search by manufacturer, model, laboratory, date range, accuracy class and compliance outcome, and exposes instrument-wise test history for repeat submissions.
Bulk export to the national Legal Metrology e-Governance portal is exposed as a versioned API/export job rather than a manual file transfer.
11.  Security, Data Integrity and Auditability
11.1  Access
 Control
Authentication is centralised in the Auth & RBAC Service; authorisation follows the role matrix defined in Section 4, enforced at the API Gateway and re-checked at each service boundary (defence in depth). Laboratory-scoped roles cannot see another laboratory's draft data; only published, signed reports are visible departmentally and to the relevant manufacturer.
11.2  Data
 Integrity
Every observation, calculation, review action and signature event is written to an append-only audit log; log entries are hash-chained so that any retroactive alteration is detectable.
Digital signatures are applied through a PKI/HSM component, isolated from the general application tier, so signing keys are never exposed to application code.
Reports are content-addressed (hashed) at signing time; the public verification portal recomputes and compares this hash to confirm a document has not been altered since issue.
11.3  Transport
 and Storage Security
TLS in transit for all client-service and inter-service communication.
Encryption at rest for the database and object storage, including photographs and supporting documents.
Secrets and signing keys held in a dedicated secrets manager / HSM, never in application configuration files.
Regular, automated, encrypted backups with tested restore procedures and a defined Recovery Point/Time Objective.
11.4  Privacy
 and Data Retention
Manufacturer and applicant data is scoped so applicants can see only their own models and reports. Retention periods for draft (unpublished) data, superseded report revisions, and audit logs are configurable per departmental record-retention policy, with archival rather than deletion as the default for anything that has entered the compliance workflow.
12.  Innovation and Global Differentiation
Building on the vision in Section 3, this section details the specific mechanisms that differentiate the proposed system from a conventional single-laboratory digitisation project.
12.1  Configuration
-First Compliance
The Rule Configuration Service is the single place OIML R-76 parameters live. A rule-authoring workflow (draft → technical review → approval → scheduled activation) lets the Legal Metrology Division stage a new OIML edition or domestic clarification ahead of its effective date, test it against sample instruments in a sandbox, and activate it without touching application code — turning a historically multi-month template-rewrite exercise into a data-governance task.
12.2  Federated
, Multi-Laboratory Operation
Rather than one deployment per laboratory, the architecture supports many laboratories on a shared platform with strict data isolation, giving the Department of Consumer Affairs the first consolidated, near-real-time national view of model approvals, laboratory throughput and compliance trends referenced in Section 1.4.
12.3  Offline
-Capable Field Client
Weighbridges and remote verification sites cannot always guarantee connectivity. The desktop client caches reference data (instrument registry, active rule version) locally, allows full data entry offline, and synchronises when connectivity returns, with conflict handling that favours laboratory-side edits and flags true conflicts for manual reconciliation.
12.4  Tamper
-Evident, Publicly Verifiable Reports
The hash-chained audit log (Section 11.2) and per-report content hash allow any party — a market surveillance officer, a consumer, a court — to verify a report's authenticity and current status (published, superseded, revoked) through a public portal without needing system access, closing a gap that purely internal digitisation efforts leave open.
12.5  Assisted
 Data-Quality Review (Roadmap)
Once sufficient historical observation data has accumulated, a later phase introduces a statistical anomaly-detection layer that flags observations inconsistent with an instrument's own test history or with peer instruments of the same model, surfacing them for reviewer attention rather than auto-rejecting them. This is explicitly a human-in-the-loop aid, never a silent override of the deterministic OIML compliance logic in Section 9.3, and is scoped to Phase 3 of the roadmap in Section 15 so that the core system does not depend on it for correctness.

Unit testing of the Calculation and Compliance Engine against a library of known-answer test cases derived from historical, manually verified reports, for every accuracy class and verification-interval band.
Rule-configuration regression testing: whenever a new OIML rule version is staged, historical reports are re-computed in a sandbox and compared against their originally issued results to confirm no unintended drift.
Integration testing across the full sequence in Figure 8.1, including both the validation-failure and reviewer-rejection loops.
Security testing: role-boundary testing to confirm laboratory data isolation, penetration testing of the API Gateway and public verification portal, and signature-verification testing.
Usability testing with practising lab technicians and approving officers, since the data-entry workflow directly affects error rates in the legal record.
Pilot validation at one designated laboratory, running the digital system in parallel with the existing manual process for an agreed period before cut-over, with discrepancies formally reconciled.

20.  Future Scope
Extend the Rule Configuration approach to other OIML recommendations (e.g., for automatic weighing instruments or other legal-metrology instrument categories), reusing the same versioned-rule and audit architecture.
Integrate with subsequent (in-service) verification workflows in the field, linking a stamped instrument back to its original type-evaluation report.
Introduce the anomaly-detection layer described in Section 12.5 once sufficient historical observation volume exists.
Support direct, calibrated-equipment data feeds (load cells, environmental sensors) as an optional input path alongside manual entry, with the Validation Engine treating both uniformly.
Explore blockchain-anchored or similarly externally-verifiable anchoring of report hashes as a further strengthening of the public verification portal in Section 12.4.
Publish anonymised, aggregate compliance-trend data to support policy research by the Legal Metrology Division.
21.  Conclusion
The current manual, spreadsheet-based approach to NAWI type-evaluation reporting under OIML R-76 is slow, error-prone, and difficult to keep current with regulatory change. This blueprint proposes a system architecture that treats compliance logic as governed, versioned configuration rather than code; that builds auditability and public verifiability into the report itself rather than bolting it on afterward; and that is designed from the outset for the connectivity realities of Indian testing laboratories and weighbridge sites. Implemented as described — with the phased roadmap in Section 15 and the domain-expert-reviewed rule encoding emphasised throughout — the system satisfies every functional requirement of Problem Statement 26035 while positioning the Department of Consumer Affairs with a national, near-real-time view of model approvals that the current process cannot provide. The team receiving this document should be able to proceed directly into detailed design and sprint planning using Sections 6 through 16 without further independent domain research, escalating only the OIML rule-encoding review in Section 9 and Section 16 to a qualified metrology advisor before any rule version is activated in production.
